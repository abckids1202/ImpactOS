# Pilar Impact Lab — School Discovery Interview Pack

**Purpose:** validate the ImpactOS V1 workflow, vocabulary, permissions, safety rules, and pilot boundary with the people who would use or govern it.

**Status:** draft for discovery; do not treat assumptions in this pack as confirmed school policy.

**Pilot name:** Pilar Impact Lab  
**Product:** ImpactOS  
**Recommended session length:** 35–50 minutes per participant  
**Research owner:** student product team, with a designated Pilar staff sponsor

---

## 1. What this research must answer

By the end of discovery, the team should be able to answer:

1. How do students currently raise concerns, find collaborators, conduct research, and report results?
2. Where do concerns or projects become stalled, duplicated, unsafe, or invisible?
3. Which terms make sense to students and adults: problem, cluster, evidence, research question, baseline, metric, intervention, impact, official response?
4. Which stages need a teacher, mentor, OSIS, moderator, or administrator checkpoint?
5. What must stay private, who can see it, and what is the urgent safeguarding route?
6. What is the smallest realistic pilot cohort and which two problem areas are appropriate for a first live test?
7. What evidence would convince Pilar that the pilot is useful and manageable?

The research is successful when it changes a product decision, a policy decision, or the pilot boundary. A list of opinions without decisions is not a completed discovery cycle.

## 2. Research principles and guardrails

- Ask about recent behavior and concrete examples before asking whether someone likes an idea.
- Do not request names or details about a real safeguarding incident in a research session.
- Remind participants that ImpactOS is not an emergency or safeguarding reporting channel.
- Do not use real student records, real sensitive reports, or identifiable survey data in prototype testing.
- Separate a participant's preference from a school policy requirement.
- Record uncertainty explicitly; use “to validate” rather than filling gaps with assumptions.
- Let participants skip any question.
- Obtain permission before recording audio or screen activity. Written notes are sufficient.

## 3. Participants and sampling

Recommended first round:

| Participant | Target | Why they matter |
|---|---:|---|
| Student contributor | 3–5 | Finds problems, signals lived experience, adds evidence |
| Student project leader | 2–3 | Owns research and intervention work |
| Teacher or mentor | 2–4 | Reviews work and carries the attention burden |
| OSIS reviewer | 1–2 | Represents student-organized priorities and updates |
| Administrator/moderator | 1–2 | Owns safety, permissions, policy, and accountability |
| Pilot sponsor/coordinator | 1 | Confirms cohort, timing, and decision authority |

Recruit variation across grade, familiarity with student projects, and comfort with digital tools. Do not recruit only the most enthusiastic students.

## 4. Session opening script

> We are exploring how Pilar currently handles student concerns and student-led projects. We are testing the workflow, not testing you. There are no right answers. Please use examples that are safe to discuss and do not share names or details about sensitive incidents. ImpactOS is not an emergency reporting channel. You may skip any question, and we will ask permission before recording anything.

Confirm:

- participant role and grade/department, without collecting unnecessary personal data;
- whether notes may include anonymized quotations;
- whether audio or screen recording is allowed;
- the correct safeguarding contact if a participant raises an urgent concern.

## 5. Common warm-up questions

Use these with everyone, then move to the role-specific guide.

1. Tell me about the last time you noticed a school or community problem worth improving.
2. What happened next? Walk me through the actual steps, tools, and people involved.
3. Where did the process feel easy? Where did it slow down or stop?
4. What information did you wish you had at the time?
5. How did you know whether the problem was real, important, or improving?
6. What would make you trust a shared school platform with this work?
7. Which words in this list feel natural or confusing: problem, evidence, research, intervention, baseline, metric, impact, response?

## 6. Student contributor guide

### Recent behavior

1. Think of a concern you raised, discussed, or chose not to raise. Where did you put it?
2. Who saw it, and how did you know it had been received?
3. Have you ever found that someone else had the same concern? How did you discover that?
4. What evidence could you realistically add: a date, observation, photo, timetable, count, or survey response?

### Safety and privacy

5. When would you want your name hidden from other students but available to a designated adult?
6. What would make a report feel too risky to submit?
7. What should the screen say if a report might involve bullying, harassment, abuse, self-harm, a crisis, or an accusation about one person?
8. Who should be able to see the report, and who should not?

### Workflow and language

9. If the system asked one or two clarification questions before submission, what would feel helpful rather than annoying?
10. Would “This affects me,” “I have evidence,” “I want to investigate,” and “I want to help” represent useful ways to respond? What is missing?
11. What would you expect to see after submitting: a status, an owner, a response date, similar concerns, or something else?
12. What would make you return to a problem page?

### Concept check

Ask the participant to explain these in their own words:

- “A problem cluster groups related reports, but a moderator decides whether reports are merged.”
- “A baseline is the measurement captured before the intervention starts.”
- “An impact page can honestly show a negative or inconclusive result.”

Probe: “What would you change in that explanation?”

## 7. Student project leader guide

### Project formation

1. How does a concern become a project today? Who decides that it is ready?
2. What makes a research question good enough to start investigating?
3. Where do you keep the plan, sources, survey, tasks, and mentor feedback?
4. Which parts are hardest to coordinate with teammates?

### Research and surveys

5. How do you choose a population, sample, and method?
6. What kinds of survey questions do you use, and how do you check that they are neutral and safe?
7. What data do you need to see as a team? What data must remain restricted?
8. What would you want an AI assistant to suggest? What must it never decide or change without you?

### Intervention and measurement

9. How do you decide what success means before building an intervention?
10. What is a realistic baseline for a student project?
11. How do you record what happened during and after the intervention?
12. What would make an impact report credible even if the result is negative or inconclusive?

### Workflow test

13. Which checkpoint would be most useful: problem validation, research plan approval, survey approval, intervention approval, or final report review?
14. Which required field would be most burdensome? What could be a template or suggestion?
15. How often would you expect a mentor to review the workspace?

## 8. Teacher or mentor guide

### Current review practice

1. What student work do you review today, and how does it arrive?
2. What makes a project ready for feedback? What usually arrives too early or too late?
3. Which issues do you catch repeatedly: vague problem, weak evidence, leading survey wording, privacy risk, no baseline, or unsupported claims?
4. How do you track requested changes and whether they were completed?

### Attention and workload

5. What would you need to see in a “needs attention” queue?
6. Which events should notify you immediately, and which can wait for a weekly digest?
7. How many active teams can you realistically support in the pilot?
8. What would make review faster without reducing judgment quality?

### Authority and safety

9. Which decisions belong to a mentor versus a moderator or administrator?
10. Who should see raw survey responses, free text, uploaded evidence, and private reports?
11. What should happen if a report or survey contains a sensitive issue?
12. What is the school's required retention, escalation, and appeal process?

### Concept and prototype feedback

13. Would “approve,” “request changes,” and “comment” match your current practice?
14. What wording would you use instead of “methodology warning” if needed?
15. Which project state should be visible to students, and which should remain internal?

## 9. OSIS reviewer guide

1. How does OSIS currently discover recurring student needs?
2. What makes a concern actionable for OSIS rather than just interesting?
3. What information can OSIS use safely at school-wide level without identifying students?
4. How should OSIS set priority factors, and what rationale must be recorded?
5. What counts as an official OSIS update? Who can publish it?
6. How should OSIS link an initiative to a problem cluster?
7. What would students need to see to believe that a concern was not ignored?
8. Which dashboards or aggregate trends would be useful weekly?
9. What topics or categories should be excluded from an OSIS-facing view?
10. How should a disagreement between OSIS and an administrator be represented?

## 10. Administrator or moderator guide

### Governance

1. Who owns private and sensitive reports from intake through resolution?
2. What is the urgent-help route shown in the product, and who monitors it?
3. Which roles can publish, merge, unmerge, archive, export, or change visibility?
4. What is the process for appeals, corrections, and takedown requests?

### Pilot configuration

5. Which grades, classes, clubs, or teams join the first pilot?
6. Which non-sensitive categories are suitable for launch?
7. Should every new report be moderated before publication during the pilot?
8. Which survey types require teacher or administrator approval?
9. How long should reports, responses, files, and audit records be retained?
10. Can any impact page be shared outside the school? Who approves it?

### Operational readiness

11. What existing account system should authentication use?
12. Who handles support, incidents, and privacy concerns during the pilot?
13. What backup and restore expectation applies to pilot data?
14. What metrics would convince school leadership to continue or stop the pilot?
15. What would make ImpactOS create unacceptable administrative work?

## 11. Artifact review exercise

Give the participant a synthetic scenario: “Students report that major assignments are often due within the same three-day period.” Do not use real Pilar data.

Ask them to place the scenario into the following sequence and explain each checkpoint:

`problem → validation → research question → survey/evidence → intervention → baseline → post-measurement → review → official response`

Capture:

- missing or unnecessary stages;
- terms the participant would rename;
- who owns each decision;
- what information is visible at each stage;
- what could cause the process to pause or route privately.

## 13. Synthesis method

Within 24 hours of each session, tag notes with:

- `workflow` — step, handoff, exception, or delay;
- `permission` — role, visibility, or decision authority;
- `safety` — privacy, moderation, safeguarding, or retention;
- `language` — misunderstood or preferred term;
- `burden` — time, review load, or operational cost;
- `value` — outcome the participant cares about;
- `pilot-boundary` — scope, cohort, category, or timing;
- `evidence` — concrete example versus aspiration.

Cluster observations only after preserving the original context. For each proposed change, record:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Observation | What the participant actually said or did |
| Confidence | High / medium / low, with reason |
| Product implication | Screen, rule, data, or workflow change |
| Policy implication | Decision Pilar must make |
| Owner | Person responsible for resolution |
| Decision date | When it must be settled |
| PRD impact | Requirement or section to update |

Do not treat a single preference as a universal requirement. Look for repeated behavior, conflicting authority, and safety-critical exceptions.

## 14. Discovery exit criteria

Discovery is ready to hand off to prototype revision when:

- at least one representative from each role has been interviewed;
- the current workflow has named tools, owners, handoffs, and failure points;
- the school has named the private-report and safeguarding owners;
- survey visibility, consent, retention, and publication rules have an accountable decision-maker;
- the pilot cohort and two candidate problem areas are identified;
- at least five product terms have been checked with students and adults;
- the five prototype tasks can be tested without using real sensitive data;
- every open decision has an owner and a target date.
